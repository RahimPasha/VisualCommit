Five candidates for the app's icon

Each mark is drawn once and shown at the sizes Windows, macOS and Linux ask for, on a dark desk and on a light one. Look first at the 16 and 24 pixel columns: that is the taskbar, the title bar and the file list, where an icon is seen most. The 256 pixel column is the largest size Windows keeps, for the big views of a folder. Tell me a letter and what to change, or that none of them is it.

The letter C, for commit, drawn as a ring around one commit. The simplest of the five, and the strongest at 16 pixels.

Watch: a ring with a dot is also how a lens, a target or a record button is drawn, so it says the least about what the app does. It leans on the accent colour to be recognised.

One commit on its history line, held in a viewfinder's corners: looking closely at a commit, which is what the app is for.

Watch: the most parts of the five. Below 24 pixels the corners and the line crowd each other, so its small sizes would be redrawn without the line. Corner brackets also mean "scan" or "screenshot" elsewhere.

What these are

First drafts, drawn by hand as vector shapes, in the app's own colours. The one you choose gets a second, simpler drawing for 16 to 32 pixels, and its edges set to whole pixels at each size.

What comes before it ships

A side-by-side check against Git's logo and the well-known Git clients' icons, so that it resembles none of them. Then the tool that makes the Windows, macOS and Linux files from the drawing.

What was left out on purpose

Git's diamond and its red, any letter G, and a full picture of a commit graph, which turns to noise at small sizes and is what several other tools already use.
